# The three-part bullet

The most common complaint about drafted resumes is that they read as a list of duties. Every accomplishment bullet answers three questions, in order:

1. **What did the user do?** Active verb, the specific thing.
2. **What did it cause?** The observable result. Not the action restated in other words.
3. **Why did that matter?** The consequence for a user, a customer, a team, or the business.

Missing part 2 makes it a job description. Missing part 3 makes the reader do the work, and a reader outside the user's field will not do it.

Model shape (made up): "Rebuilt the returns checklist with the warehouse leads; mis-shipped returns fell by half, which freed a weekend shift for restocking."

## The duty test

Cover the first clause. If what remains doesn't say what changed, it's a duty. Two reliable tells: present-tense "Own / Lead / Support / Run / Manage" with no second clause, and a bullet that lists what something contained rather than what it produced.

## Exceptions

- One scope-setting lead bullet per role ("Lead ... for ...").
- Every bullet finishes its own thought. No "the platform", "the pilot", or "it" leaning on the bullet before, because tailored pages pick bullets one at a time.

## Other failure modes

- Table stakes: the definitional result of a required process belongs in the profile or an interview answer, not a bullet.
- Status endings ("added to the plan", "discussed with the team"). Strong bullets end on a result: risk avoided, time freed, a customer result, or a decision someone made.
- Cause and effect that don't connect. If the "so" hangs off the how instead of the decision, the reader can't follow.
- Counting access as use. People who could use something are not people who use it.
- Finished-work verbs for unfinished work. "Leading", "developing", "scoped" for work in progress; never "migrated" or "shut down" until it's done.

## Length and shape

Sentence shape follows the user's Rules File (sections 2 and 3). Split anything past about 45 words, always past 80. Cut any clause that re-explains a term already used in the bullet.

## When a part is missing

If part 2 or 3 is missing because Claude doesn't know it, ask the user. Never invent one, and never append a plausible-sounding clause to a true one. A fabrication next to a fact is still a fabrication.

## Approving tighter wording

When the user asks for tighter bullets, send one numbered list with the proposed text and word count for each, keep every cleared figure and all three parts, and apply only what they approve, in evidence mode. Never tighten wording inside an application.
